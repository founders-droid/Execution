# Strategic differentiation (how to endure)

## From the course

Value proposition and strategic differentiation are often confused or even used as synonyms, so it's worth quickly clarifying the difference between the two.

Whereas value proposition describes what solution you provide, strategic differentiation describes how you keep that value proposition compelling against your competitors.

For example, LinkedIn's value proposition was a 'warmer, higher-success-rate way to do sales,' but its strategic differentiation was its data. Competitors could not easily copy LinkedIn's ability to constantly keep its massive database of users up-to-date and accurate.

When defining this dimension, the surface level understanding most PMs stop at is to think only about their strategic differentiation against direct competitors.

However, this misses the fact that there are often indirect alternatives users are always considering, as well as adjacent markets that are building into your functionality.

As a result, you may end up creating a compelling product against direct competition, but not be compelling enough to capture another large market of users who are using indirect alternatives.

Similarly, you may not be actively defending against encroaching adjacent markets.

For example, if Substack only considered other publishing platforms like Wordpress, Medium, or Ghost as competition, it might be tempted to simply build a publishing platform.

However, building an app solely to beat existing publishing platforms means Substack might not differentiate itself very well against the 'full field of competition' that it faces. It would miss out on the opportunity to potentially capture writers from indirect alternatives…

### What this means for a critique
- Check that the strategy **doesn't confuse value proposition with differentiation**. "We solve X better" is a value proposition; differentiation explains why competitors can't catch up.
- Map the **full field of competition** and flag any that are missing:
  - **Direct competitors:** products that do the same job the same way.
  - **Indirect alternatives:** other ways customers solve the problem today (spreadsheets, advisors, doing nothing).
  - **Adjacent markets:** players in neighboring categories building into this functionality.
- Name the specific **hard-to-copy asset**, like LinkedIn's constantly updated data.

## Critique checklist
Strategic differentiation answers: **why will we keep winning despite fast-moving competition?** A good value proposition wins customers today; differentiation protects that win once competitors copy it.

### What great looks like
- Names **durable advantages (moats)** that strengthen over time, e.g.:
  - **Network effects:** the product gets better as more people use it.
  - **Proprietary data:** usage creates data competitors can't get.
  - **Switching costs:** customers' data, workflows, or integrations make leaving painful.
  - **Scale economies:** unit costs fall faster than competitors' do.
  - **Brand and trust:** especially in high-stakes categories.
  - **Unique assets:** distribution, partnerships, regulatory position, technology.
- Explains **how the moat is built step by step**, starting from zero customers.
- Shows **honest awareness of competitors**, both current and likely entrants, and how they'd respond.

### Red flags to call out
- "Better UX", "first mover", or "we'll execute faster" as the only advantage.
- An advantage an incumbent or a well-funded startup could copy in months.
- Features presented as a moat.
- Out-of-date or incomplete competitor landscape; missing the closest direct competitors.
- The moat only exists at a scale the strategy has no plan to reach.

### Questions to ask
- If a well-funded competitor copied our product tomorrow, why would customers stay?
- Which advantage gets stronger with each new customer?
- Who are the 3 closest competitors today, and what will they do in response?
