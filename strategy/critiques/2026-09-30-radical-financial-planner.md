# Strategy critique: The Radical Financial Planner
- **Date:** 2026-09-30 · **Verdict:** Ready with fixes (Problem & Target Audience only; rest not yet reviewed)
- **Source:** Screenshot excerpt: "Problem We Are Solving" and "Target Audience" (primary segment). Later sections were not visible.

## The strategy in 3 bullets
- **Who:** Dual-income professionals, ages 30–50, with $150K–$500K household income, who already track their money but lack time to maintain complex spreadsheets.
- **Problem:** No tool or advisor connects today's decisions (house, kids, career, retirement) to lifetime financial outcomes. The result is anxiety and decision paralysis.
- **Bet:** Automated, personal, lifetime financial modeling will beat spending trackers (Mint, Empower, Quicken) and advisors, who are described as too simple, too static, or too expensive.

## Top weaknesses & fixes
1. **Competitive framing is outdated and misses the real rivals.**
   - The strategy argues against trackers, but the true alternatives for this segment are lifetime planners (ProjectionLab, Boldin), advisor-built plans, and DIY spreadsheets.
   - **Fix:** Redo the landscape using those alternatives. State one sharp reason to win, such as "no manual upkeep: the plan updates itself from linked accounts" or "answers decisions, not just projections".
   - **Ask:** "Why would a ProjectionLab or Boldin user switch, and why would a spreadsheet user pay?"
2. **The pain is asserted, not evidenced, and willingness to pay is unknown.**
   - A high-income segment can already pay an advisor. The claim that advisors are "too expensive" needs proof for people earning $150K–$500K.
   - **Fix:** Add evidence, for example: "X of Y interviewees made a major decision in the last 12 months without modeling it", plus current spend on advisors or tools.
   - **Ask:** "What do target users pay today for planning, and what did they last decide without a plan?"
3. **The problem is episodic, which is a retention risk.**
   - Big decisions (home, kids, retirement date) happen a few times a year or less. A planner that's used once and then abandoned won't sustain a subscription.
   - **Fix:** Define a recurring value loop, such as a monthly "on track / off track" check, alerts when reality drifts from the plan, and quick "can I afford X?" scenarios.
   - **Fix:** Choose a North Star that reflects ongoing value. Examples: monthly active planners, or decisions modeled per user per quarter.
   - **Ask:** "Why does someone open this in a month when nothing big is happening?"

**Also worth fixing:**
- Pick one beachhead segment, either dual-income professionals or young families, and name the other as V2.
- Define "Radical": what's radically different, in one sentence?

## Riskiest assumptions
| Assumption | Why risky | Cheapest test |
|---|---|---|
| Users will trust an automated forecast for six-figure decisions | Trust is the core barrier in personal finance; one bad projection costs credibility | Concierge test: build plans by hand for 10 target users and measure how many act on them within 30 days |
| The segment will pay a subscription instead of using free tools, spreadsheets, or an advisor | Free and low-cost alternatives exist | Fake-door pricing page or paid pre-orders: aim for ≥ 5% of visitors to click through to checkout |
| Users will return after the initial setup | The problem is episodic (see weakness 3) | Clickable prototype plus a monthly check-in email; measure the share of users who return within 60 days |
| Personalized guidance can be offered without registering as an investment adviser | Regulatory scope drives cost and what the product may say | One-hour consult with a fintech or securities lawyer |
| Linked-account data is complete enough to model from | Missing 401(k), equity, or real-estate data breaks the forecast | Plaid sandbox pilot with 20 users; measure the share of net worth captured automatically |

## What's strong
- The problem is framed around **decisions** (house, retirement, time off work), not dashboards. That's a clear, emotionally resonant job-to-be-done.
- The primary segment is concrete and reachable: high income, analytical, time-poor, and already tracking their money.
- "Accessible, automated, deeply personal" is a usable design principle, as long as it's backed by a specific edge over competitors.
